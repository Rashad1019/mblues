I
IntroductionMemphis, 2023

This Did Not
Happen Overnight

Memphis, Tennessee has, for much of the last forty years, ranked among the most violent large cities in America. In 2023 the city recorded 397 homicides, a modern record and, per capita, one of the worst years any American city of its size has experienced in recent memory. Carjackings became so frequent and so brazen they made national news weekly. The Department of Justice opened a pattern-and-practice investigation into the Memphis Police Department. National Guard troops were eventually deployed. By any measure, the first half of the 2020s represented a full-scale urban safety crisis.

Your question is the right one. This did not happen overnight.

The violence we see in Memphis in our lifetimes is the accumulated consequence of more than two hundred years of economic, racial, political, and public-policy decisions. Some of those decisions were made by slaveholders. Some were made by Congress. Some were made by federal housing administrators drawing colored lines on maps in 1935. Some were made by factory owners moving their plants overseas in the 1980s. Some were made by local politicians in the 1990s pursuing "tough on crime" policies that broke up the very families whose stability is the strongest predictor of low crime. And many were made by no single person at all. They are the emergent result of a city built, from its founding, on extraction: the extraction of labor, wealth, and opportunity from its Black majority for the benefit of a mostly white minority.

"Violence is a symptom. The disease runs deep."

This report traces that chain of cause and effect from before Memphis's 1819 founding all the way to the present. It will not try to absolve any individual of the choices they made. Memphis has produced more than its share of both perpetrators and victims of individual violence. But it will insist, throughout, on a simple claim: that you cannot understand why a 19-year-old on the corner of Lamar and Willett picked up a gun in 2022 without understanding why his great-great-grandmother was denied a mortgage in 1938, why her son lost his Firestone job in 1983, and why his son spent seven years in state prison for a crime that carries a fine in most states now. Violence is a symptom. The disease runs deep.

I
Chapter I1541 1819

Before Memphis:
The Chickasaw Bluff

The fourth Chickasaw Bluff, the geological formation on which downtown Memphis now sits, was not empty land waiting to be settled. It had been continuously inhabited for roughly 10,000 years. By about 800 CE, the Mississippian culture, the sophisticated mound-building civilization that also built Cahokia in Illinois, had established a chain of towns stretching roughly 40 miles along this stretch of the Mississippi River, including the settlement now preserved as the Chucalissa archaeological site in southwest Memphis. By the time Europeans arrived, the dominant Native nation in the region was the Chickasaw, who controlled the bluffs and the surrounding territory through a network of villages, hunting grounds, and trade routes.

Spanish conquistador Hernando de Soto is believed to have crossed the Mississippi near Memphis in 1541; his encounter with the Chickasaws ended badly for him. The Chickasaws burned his camp, destroyed much of his equipment, seized hundreds of his hogs, and drove him out. He died the following year without returning. French explorers followed in the 1670s. Rene-Robert Cavelier, Sieur de La Salle, built Fort Prudhomme near the bluffs in 1682 during his descent of the Mississippi. This was the first European structure in what would become the Memphis area, predating Anglo-American settlement in East Tennessee by more than 70 years.

In the 1730s, the French empire decided the Chickasaws, who had been allying with English traders, needed to be eliminated. What followed was the Chickasaw War, the largest military undertaking in the history of French Louisiana. In 1739, Jean-Baptiste Le Moyne, Sieur de Bienville, led an army of 1,200 Frenchmen (joined by roughly 2,400 Black and Indigenous auxiliary troops) to the fourth Chickasaw Bluff with the explicit goal of eradicating the Chickasaw nation. On August 15, 1739, the Feast of the Assumption, they completed construction of Fort Assumption on the bluff, at what is now downtown Memphis. The campaign was a disaster. Through the winter of 1739 and 1740, Bienville's army was devastated by weather, disease, desertion, and drunkenness. The Chickasaws, though outnumbered, held the field. By March 1740 the French withdrew, released their hostages, and abandoned the fort. The fourth Chickasaw Bluff was a site of attempted ethnic cleansing before it was a city.

British control followed the French and Indian War in 1763. After the American Revolution, control passed to the state of North Carolina, then to Tennessee on statehood in 1796. But actual sovereignty remained with the Chickasaw Nation. The land was theirs by treaty and by possession. That moment came in the Chickasaw Cession of 1818 (often called the Jackson Purchase), negotiated by Andrew Jackson and Isaac Shelby under heavy pressure and with significant bribery of Chickasaw leaders. The Chickasaws ceded all their land west of the Tennessee River. A year later, Jackson, along with his partners John Overton and James Winchester, laid out the town of Memphis on bluffs they had personally bought on speculation. The city was named after the ancient capital of Egypt, a gesture of grandiosity that revealed the founders' ambition but had no organic connection to the place they were naming.

"From its first day, Memphis was a site of dispossession repurposed as a commercial opportunity for a small group of well-connected white men. That basic pattern would define almost everything that followed."

A City Built on
the Slave Trade

The city's geography determined its destiny. It sat at the northernmost point on the Mississippi where the river did not flood extensively, giving it a deepwater port that could operate in any season. Any crop grown within a 200-mile radius could be hauled to Memphis, loaded onto a steamboat, and sent to New Orleans for export. The city was, from birth, a funnel. In the 1820s and 1830s, Memphis was actually somewhat ambivalent about slavery. The cotton boom erased all of that. In 1830, about 1,000 bales of cotton passed through Memphis. Ten years later, that number had exploded to 35,000 bales worth $1.4 million. Cotton demanded labor. Labor, under the plantation system, meant enslaved human beings.

In 1854, the Tennessee legislature repealed the ban on the interstate importation of enslaved people for sale. Almost overnight, Memphis was transformed into one of the largest slave markets in the entire world. By 1857, it sat alongside Washington, Richmond, Charleston, Savannah, and New Orleans as a central hub of the American interstate slave trade. Most of the Memphis slave trade was concentrated on Adams Avenue, a street that, strategically, connected the river steamboat landing to the Memphis and Charleston Railroad line. In 1855, the Memphis city directory listed eight slave dealers, five of them on Adams. By 1860, the number had grown to ten dealers, six on Adams. In addition to the roughly 3,000 enslaved people who lived and worked in Memphis year-round, thousands more flowed through the city each year, transient captives warehoused in pens before being sold south to the Delta plantations.

The most infamous of the Memphis slave traders was Nathan Bedford Forrest, whose pen stood at 87 Adams Street, literally across the alley from Calvary Episcopal Church. Forrest ran his operation from 1854 to 1860. By the best available estimate, roughly 3,800 human beings were trafficked through Forrest's jail alone in those six years. Forrest was remarkable even among slave traders for dealing in Africans who had been illegally smuggled into the United States in violation of the 1808 federal ban on the international slave trade. In January 1860, his three-story slave pen at 89 Adams literally collapsed under the weight of the people inside it. Two enslaved people were killed and four more injured. Bills of sale for $400,000 worth of human "property" (about $14 million in 2025 dollars) were salvaged from the wreckage.

Forrest would go on to serve as a Confederate lieutenant general, commit what historians overwhelmingly consider a war crime at the Fort Pillow massacre of Black Union soldiers, and become one of the early leaders of the Ku Klux Klan.

It is important to name what is often elided in textbook histories of slavery. The Memphis slave market placed a premium on young enslaved women, both as breeders to reproduce the enslaved population and as sexual objects for purchasers. Historical research based on price records shows that young women capable of bearing children consistently fetched higher prices. This dimension of the trade, systematic and commercialized sexual exploitation, is a founding trauma of Black Memphis.

It is tempting to put slavery in a box and call it the past. It is more accurate to say that Memphis's institutions, its banks, its newspapers, its churches, its police force, its political elite, its physical downtown, were built, shaped, and financed by the buying and selling of human beings and the extraction of unpaid labor from them. When slavery ended in 1865, those institutions did not disappear. They adapted. And the essential logic that underwrote them, that a white propertied class would extract wealth from a Black laboring class by violence if necessary, would persist in one form or another for another 100 years.

III
Chapter VMay 1 3, 1866

The Memphis Massacre

Not a riot. Not a clash. A massacre.

When the Civil War ended in April 1865, roughly 20,000 formerly enslaved people were living in or around Memphis. Most were crowded into South Memphis, near Fort Pickering. At the same time, the city's Irish immigrant population had grown rapidly, and many Irish men were employed as Memphis police officers and firefighters, jobs that placed them in direct daily competition with, and authority over, freedpeople seeking work and housing. White planters wanted freedpeople driven out of Memphis and back onto plantations. Ex-Confederates wanted to humiliate Black veterans. And newly freed people, especially Union veterans stationed at Fort Pickering, refused to accept second-class citizenship after having literally fought and won a war.

On April 30, 1866, white Memphis police officers on Causey Street forced a group of Black men off the sidewalk; a confrontation followed. The next afternoon, on May 1, a crowd of Black men, many of them recently discharged Union soldiers, assembled on South Street. Someone fired. What followed was three days of organized racial terror. Not a riot, not a clash, but a massacre. The Memphis police force itself participated. City Recorder John Creighton gave a speech early in the violence explicitly calling on the white crowd to arm and go kill the Black people and drive them from the city.

Five Black women testified before a congressional committee about sexual violence committed against them by white mobs and police officers, making them among the first African American women ever to testify before Congress about sexual assault. Frances Thompson, a Black woman who had been enslaved in Maryland before moving to Memphis, where she worked as a seamstress and washerwoman, described seven white men forcing their way into her home on Gayoso Street on the night of May 1, 1866. They demanded dinner; Thompson cooked for them. Then they demanded sex; when she and her 16-year-old housemate, Lucy Smith, refused, the men drew their pistols, beat and choked both women, and raped them. The attackers also robbed the two women of about $300 (roughly $6,600 in 2025 dollars) and the silk dresses they owned. Lucy Smith, 16 years old, testified that she was so badly injured she believed she had been killed.

Historians now recognize Thompson and Smith, along with Lucy Tibbs, Elvira Walker, and Rebecca Bloom, as foundational figures in American civil rights history. Their testimony provided the evidence that helped move Congress to pass the Fourteenth Amendment. Recent scholarship has established that Frances Thompson was a transgender woman, making her the first known trans woman to testify before the United States Congress. She was targeted, outed, and persecuted for her gender identity a decade later by the same white-supremacist press that wanted to discredit her 1866 testimony.

"Not a single person was ever prosecuted for the Memphis Massacre. Not for the murders. Not for the rapes. Not for the arson. This was the pattern that would define Memphis for a century."

The national public was horrified. The congressional report, combined with a similar massacre in New Orleans in July 1866, shifted public opinion decisively. The Fourteenth Amendment, guaranteeing birthright citizenship, equal protection, and due process, passed Congress in June 1866 and was ratified in 1868, in substantial part as a response to the Memphis Massacre. The Reconstruction Acts, which placed the South under federal military supervision, followed in 1867. But not a single person was ever prosecuted for the Memphis Massacre. Not for the murders. Not for the rapes. Not for the arson. Not for the robberies. Federal troops remained in the South for a decade, but the political will in the North to sustain Reconstruction eroded. By the Compromise of 1877, federal troops were withdrawn, and Reconstruction was abandoned. Within a generation, almost every right African Americans had won was rolled back. The Fourteenth Amendment became, in practice, a dead letter for 90 years.

Yellow Fever and the
Reshaping of the City

Yellow fever, spread by the Aedes aegypti mosquito (though nobody at the time understood this), had struck Memphis episodically for decades. But the 1870s were catastrophic. The 1873 epidemic killed roughly 2,000 Memphians. And then came 1878. In the summer of 1878, yellow fever exploded in New Orleans and began moving up the Mississippi River on steamboats. Memphis tried to quarantine itself, but a steamboat worker named William Warren slipped past the checkpoints and ate at a riverfront restaurant owned by Kate Bionda on August 1. He died shortly afterward. Kate Bionda died on August 13, becoming the first confirmed Memphis case.

Panic followed. Within two weeks, roughly 25,000 of the city's 50,000 residents fled, overwhelmingly the middle-class and wealthy, overwhelmingly white. Of the approximately 20,000 who remained, an estimated 17,000 contracted the fever. 5,150 died. At the peak, 200 people were dying every day. Half of the city's physicians died. Funeral bells rang almost continuously. The mayor was dead. The sheriff, city marshal, and deputies were dead or gone.

The death rate divided sharply by race. Of roughly 6,000 whites who remained, nearly all contracted the fever, and 4,204 died, a catastrophic mortality rate. Of the roughly 14,000 Black residents who remained, 11,000 contracted the fever, but only 946 died, likely the result of genetic resistance inherited from generations of ancestral exposure to yellow fever in West Africa.

The epidemic fundamentally remade Memphis in five major ways:

  • 01The tax base collapsed. In 1879 the Tennessee legislature revoked the city's charter. Memphis operated as a state-run "taxing district" for 14 years, unable to levy taxes or issue bonds independently.
  • 02The population mix shifted permanently. The German and Irish Catholic communities, which had been economically rising, were devastated and never fully recovered. The old antebellum planter class lost most of its remaining survivors.
  • 03Black Memphians briefly gained political power. Because so many white residents had died or fled, Black men were briefly placed on the police force as patrolmen almost unheard of in the Reconstruction-era South. Black officers would remain on the Memphis force until 1895, when the door slammed shut again.
  • 04A new, harder white elite took control. The business-class survivors were, by and large, more rigidly white-supremacist and more politically conservative than the pre-fever generation. They would reimpose Jim Crow in the 1890s with extraordinary severity.
  • 05Public health infrastructure was finally built. Memphis built a sewer system, cleaned up its open privies, and tapped the artesian aquifer that still provides its drinking water today. These reforms saved countless future lives built by and for a city that had been demographically and economically gutted.
After yellow fever, Memphis was "twentieth-century in externals, Confederate at heart." Gerald Capers, 1938
V
Chapter VII1880s 1920s

Jim Crow, Ida B. Wells,
and the Lynching Regime

By the late 1880s, white Memphians were systematically reimposing a racial caste system. Black men were pushed off juries, disenfranchised through poll taxes and literacy tests, and squeezed out of skilled trades. Segregation became law and custom. And the enforcement mechanism, the thing that ensured no Black Memphian could forget his or her place, was lynching.

On the night of March 9, 1892, three Black Memphis businessmen, Thomas Moss, Calvin McDowell, and William Stewart, were dragged from the Shelby County jail and lynched by a white mob. Their crime was competing successfully with a white-owned grocery store across the street. The People's Grocery, located in the Curve neighborhood, was a Black-owned, Black-operated business, and it was taking customers away from its white competitor. Thomas Moss was a close friend and the godfather of the child of a young Memphis journalist named Ida B. Wells.

Wells had been born into slavery in Holly Springs, Mississippi in 1862 and moved to Memphis as a young woman, where she became co-owner and editor of the Memphis Free Speech and Headlight, a Black-owned newspaper. The People's Grocery lynching radicalized her. She began investigating lynchings across the South, personally traveling to lynching sites, interviewing witnesses, and reading coroners' reports. Her findings, published in her pamphlet Southern Horrors: Lynch Law in All Its Phases (1892) and later The Red Record (1895), destroyed the prevailing white narrative that lynching was a response to Black men raping white women. Wells documented, with data, that most lynching victims had not been accused of rape at all, and that many "rape" accusations involved consensual interracial relationships.

On May 27, 1892, while Wells was in New York, a white mob destroyed the offices of the Free Speech. She was warned that if she returned to Memphis, she would be killed. She never returned. She continued her anti-lynching crusade from Chicago, where she helped found the NAACP in 1909 and became one of the foundational figures of American civil rights journalism.

Twenty-five years later, Memphis lynching remained a public spectacle. In May 1917, a Black woodcutter named Ell Persons was accused, without meaningful evidence, of murdering a 16-year-old white girl. He was seized from law enforcement custody by a mob of several thousand white Memphians. He was taken to a clearing near the Wolf River, doused with gasoline, and burned alive. The crowd numbered more than 5,000 people. Some brought their children. Food was sold. After he was dead, his body was dismembered; his head was reportedly thrown from a car onto Beale Street, the center of Black Memphis. No one was prosecuted. Ell Persons's lynching was one of the events that prompted the NAACP to launch the silent protest parade of July 28, 1917 in New York City, one of the first civil rights mass marches in American history.

"Black Memphis's cultural richness and Black Memphis's brutal economic and physical conditions are not two different stories. They are the same story."

Against this backdrop of terror, Black Memphians built an astonishing culture of their own. Beale Street, a thoroughfare running east from the river through Black downtown, became by the 1910s and 1920s one of the most important Black commercial and cultural districts in the country. Robert R. Church Sr., born enslaved in 1839, became one of the first Black millionaires in the American South and built much of the Beale Street Black business district. W.C. Handy, "the Father of the Blues," composed "Memphis Blues" (1912) and "Beale Street Blues" (1916) in and about the neighborhood. The blues as a musical genre was substantially invented by Black Memphians (Handy, Memphis Minnie, Furry Lewis, and dozens of others) as a response to the conditions under which they lived. This is a crucial point that often gets lost: Black Memphis's cultural richness and Black Memphis's brutal economic and physical conditions are not two different stories. They are the same story. The music industry that would later produce Stax Records, Al Green, Isaac Hayes, and Three 6 Mafia was built by people who were simultaneously being lynched, disenfranchised, redlined, and underpaid.

VI
Chapter VIII1909 1954

The Crump Machine

From roughly 1909 until his death in 1954, Memphis was effectively ruled by one man: Edward Hull "Boss" Crump. Crump became mayor in 1910 and never really let go, even after he was forced out of the mayor's office on a technicality in 1915. Through a combination of patronage, voter manipulation, and old-fashioned bribery, he ran one of the most powerful urban political machines in American history, on par with Tammany Hall in New York or the Daley machine in Chicago.

The Crump machine was unusual among Southern political machines in that it actively courted Black votes. Crump knew that Memphis's Black population was large enough that whoever controlled Black turnout controlled city elections. He made deals with Black political brokers (including, for a time, Robert R. Church Jr., son of the pioneering Black businessman), paid Black voters' poll taxes in exchange for their votes, and ensured that Black ballots were counted, at least when they were voting for his machine's candidates.

But this was not a civil rights machine. Crump enforced segregation rigidly. Black Memphians had access to limited patronage and protection from the random violence of the white underclass, but they had no real political power, no meaningful economic power, no integrated schools, no integrated public accommodations, and no seat at any table that mattered.

"Our advantage is the Negro and there is no race problem at all." Memphis Business Men's Club, 1900

The Crump machine was also aggressively hostile to labor unions. Memphis in the 1930s and 1940s was notorious nationally as one of the most anti-union cities in America. Union organizers, Black or white, were beaten, jailed, or driven out of town. Crump's police force broke strikes with brutal efficiency. This mattered enormously for the trajectory of the city: while industrial cities in the North and Midwest were building mass labor movements that would lift millions of working-class families into the middle class, Memphis systematically suppressed the organized working class.

The ideology behind this was old. In 1900, the all-white Business Men's Club had told a reporter for The Commercial Appeal that "our advantage is the Negro...and there is no race problem at all." They meant that low Black wages were Memphis's competitive edge in attracting capital. That logic, that Memphis's prosperity depended on cheap, unorganized, disproportionately Black labor, was institutionalized under Crump and has never really been dislodged. In 2017, the region's economy was estimated to be $22 billion smaller than it would have been without the Black to white wage gap.

VII
Chapter IX1910 1960

The Great Migration and
the Building of Black Memphis

6M
African Americans who left the rural South between 1910 and 1970

Between roughly 1910 and 1970, some six million African Americans left the rural South in what historians call the Great Migration. Memphis was, famously, a way station in this migration. Many Black Mississippians and Arkansans stopped in Memphis, some permanently, some for a generation, before moving on to Chicago, Detroit, or St. Louis.

But many stayed. The Black population of Memphis grew steadily throughout the first half of the 20th century. Black neighborhoods like Orange Mound (founded in the 1890s as one of the first Black neighborhoods in America built by and for African Americans), South Memphis, North Memphis, and Binghamton were vibrant, densely populated communities with their own schools, churches, newspapers, hospitals, businesses, and culture.

The contradiction of this era is important to name clearly. Black Memphis was, by the 1940s and 1950s, larger and more institutionally developed than it had ever been. And yet it was almost entirely excluded from the economic mainstream of the city. Most Black Memphians worked as domestic servants, laborers, cotton handlers (Memphis was the world's largest cotton market into the 1950s), or sharecroppers in the surrounding Delta who cycled in and out of the city. A small Black middle class of teachers, ministers, morticians, and doctors served the community but remained walled off from broader economic opportunity by segregation. They could build institutions, churches, mutual aid societies, insurance companies, newspapers, but they could not access federal mortgage programs, join most unions, or compete on equal terms for industrial employment. The wall was legal, administrative, and enforced by violence.

"Black Memphis had built a world inside the walls. What it could never do under Jim Crow, under Crump, under the full weight of the American state was break through them."

Chapter X1935 1968

Redlining and the
Engineering of the
Racial Wealth Gap

To understand Memphis violence today, you have to understand what the federal government did to Memphis's neighborhoods in the 1930s.

Starting in 1935, the federal Home Owners' Loan Corporation (HOLC) drew color-coded maps of every American city with a population over 40,000, more than 240 cities in all. HOLC graded each neighborhood on a four-tier scale. Grade A (green) neighborhoods were the "best": typically all-white, affluent, and suburban. Grade B (blue) neighborhoods were "still desirable." Grade C (yellow) neighborhoods were "definitely declining." Grade D (red) neighborhoods were labeled "hazardous," with no loans recommended. The grades were drawn by local white real estate agents, bankers, and insurers. Unsurprisingly, every predominantly Black neighborhood in Memphis was graded Red. Most racially mixed or working-class white neighborhoods were graded Yellow.

The consequences were enormous. The Federal Housing Administration (FHA), established in 1934, used maps like these to decide which mortgages it would insure. Private banks used them to decide where to lend. The practical effect was that federally subsidized, low-interest, 30-year mortgages flowed almost exclusively to white neighborhoods in the eastern and suburban parts of Memphis, and almost not at all to Black neighborhoods in North and South Memphis. This was not a byproduct of market forces. It was federal policy.

"This was not a byproduct of market forces. It was federal policy."

For the next 30 years and more, the basic mechanism of American middle-class wealth-building, homeownership with federally subsidized mortgages, with the house appreciating in value over decades, was made available to white Memphians and systematically denied to Black Memphians. Meanwhile, the neighborhoods that had been redlined suffered cascading consequences. Banks stopped making loans there, so homes deteriorated. Insurance companies stopped writing policies, so property owners could not recover from fires or storms. Real estate agents engaged in "blockbusting," warning white residents that property values would fall if more Black families moved in, buying homes at artificially depressed prices, and reselling them to Black families at inflated prices. As homes deteriorated and families could not refinance, disinvestment spread. Businesses left. Grocery stores closed. Employers relocated. Schools lost tax-base funding.

Across the United States, the average Black family today holds roughly one-tenth the wealth of the average white family. This gap is not primarily about differences in income or work ethic or savings behavior. It is primarily about differences in home equity and inherited wealth, and those differences are primarily the result of redlining and its consequences, passed down across three generations. In Memphis today, Black homeownership stands at roughly 42%, compared to roughly 72% for white Memphians. Black Memphis homeownership actually declined 18% between 2005 and 2017.

A child growing up one mile away in Midtown has roughly a 16% chance. That gap was engineered, deliberately, by federal policy nearly 90 years ago and it has never been repaired.

VIII
Chapter XIFebruary April 1968
I AMA MAN.

On February 1, 1968, a cold, rainy day, two Black Memphis sanitation workers, Echol Cole (age 36) and Robert Walker (age 29), took shelter from a downpour inside the barrel of their garbage truck. They had no choice. Under the city's rules, Black sanitation workers who stopped working during rain received no pay, while white supervisors went home with full pay. And Black sanitation workers were not permitted to take shelter in the public buildings where white workers could wait out the rain. The truck's compactor malfunctioned. Cole and Walker were crushed to death. Two other men had died the same way in 1964. The city had been asked repeatedly to take the defective trucks out of service. It had refused. Cole and Walker had no insurance and no pension. Their families were left destitute.

On February 12, 1968, 1,300 Black Memphis sanitation workers went on strike. Their demands: recognition of their union (AFSCME Local 1733), a living wage, safer equipment, an end to the "rainy day" policy, and the basic dignity of being treated as human beings rather than disposable labor. Their slogan, printed on placards they carried through downtown, became one of the defining images of 20th-century America: I AM A MAN.

Mayor Henry Loeb refused to negotiate. He refused to recognize the union. He refused to hear the City Council's attempts to broker a settlement. On February 23, Memphis police attacked a nonviolent march on City Hall with tear gas and mace. The city's Black community was radicalized. Daily marches began. Over 100 people, including dozens of ministers, were arrested. James Lawson, King's close friend and longtime co-worker in the nonviolent movement, asked Dr. Martin Luther King Jr. to come to Memphis. King, at the time, was organizing the Poor People's Campaign. He saw Memphis as the Poor People's Campaign in microcosm. King arrived on March 18, 1968, and spoke to a rally of 15,000 to 25,000 people at Bishop Charles Mason Temple, reportedly the largest indoor civil rights gathering ever held.

"On April 3, King returned. In a storm, at Mason Temple, he delivered the 'Mountaintop' speech. He said he might not get to the Promised Land with them, but that they would get there as a people."

On April 3, 1968, King returned to Memphis. That evening, in a storm, at Mason Temple, he delivered what would become one of the most famous speeches of the 20th century, the "Mountaintop" speech. He spoke about threats against his life; he spoke about what God had allowed him to see; he said he might not get to the Promised Land with them, but that they would get there as a people. The next evening, on April 4, 1968, King was standing on the balcony of the Lorraine Motel on Mulberry Street when a single rifle shot, fired from a boarding house across the way by escaped convict James Earl Ray, struck him in the jaw and severed his spinal cord. He died about an hour later at St. Joseph's Hospital.

Riots broke out in more than 100 American cities. In Memphis, the strike continued. On April 8, Coretta Scott King led a silent march of 42,000 people through downtown Memphis. On April 16, the city finally agreed to recognize AFSCME Local 1733 and grant the workers' wage demands. The strike was over. The workers had won.

But something else had ended too. King's assassination shattered the civil rights coalition at the national level. It also, in Memphis specifically, broke something. The city's white population had overwhelmingly sided with Mayor Loeb against the strikers. After King's murder, and after the 1968 riots that followed it, the pace of white flight accelerated dramatically. Downtown businesses closed. Department stores relocated to the suburbs. The idea that Memphis could ever be an integrated, mutually thriving city took a blow it has arguably never recovered from.

IX
Chapter XII1970s 1990s

Deindustrialization, White
Flight, and the Hollowing
of Memphis

The two decades after King's assassination were, for Memphis's working class (Black and white, but disproportionately Black), an economic disaster.

Between roughly 1970 and the early 2000s, Memphis lost most of the industrial base that had been the backbone of its 20th-century economy. Firestone Tire and Rubber Company, one of the city's largest employers of Black workers and a unionized operation that had been a path into the Black middle class, closed its Memphis plant in 1983. International Harvester (farm equipment) closed. The Memphis Furniture Manufacturing Company closed. Television assembly (RCA, then Sharp) left. Food processing plants consolidated and left. Agricultural implements and processing declined with mechanization.

These were not marginal jobs. They were unionized industrial jobs, the kind of jobs that paid $15 to $25 an hour in 1970s dollars, with benefits, pensions, and the prospect of a middle-class life for the worker's family. They were the exact kind of jobs Dr. King had been fighting to expand and protect for Black workers.

When they disappeared, they were replaced largely by low-wage, non-union service jobs: warehousing, hospitality, fast food, and, as Memphis increasingly branded itself as "America's Distribution Center," the FedEx and logistics sector. FedEx was founded in Little Rock in 1971 and moved its hub to Memphis International Airport in 1973. Today it is the city's largest employer. But logistics work, especially entry-level package-handler work, pays a fraction of what a Firestone job paid in 1975 adjusted for inflation, and has far weaker union protection.

Beginning in the 1950s and accelerating sharply after 1968, middle-class white Memphians left the city in enormous numbers. They moved to Whitehaven (until it, too, integrated, after which they kept moving), then to Raleigh, Frayser, East Memphis, and eventually to independent suburban municipalities: Germantown, Collierville, Bartlett, Lakeland, Arlington. Many more moved across the state line into DeSoto County, Mississippi, to Olive Branch, Southaven, Horn Lake, Hernando, a classic "fiscal wall" migration that let white families access Memphis jobs while paying taxes to and sending their children to schools in a different jurisdiction.

Memphis responded to white flight initially with aggressive annexation. By the early 2000s, Memphis's land area was larger than Pittsburgh, Cleveland, Cincinnati, and Atlanta combined. It did not work. It stretched the city's services thinly across a massive footprint, and eventually Memphis began to de-annex, giving up Eads, Southwind/Windyke, South Cordova, and Rocky Point, losing about 11,000 residents. By the 2000s, Memphis City Schools was roughly 85% Black and chronically underfunded. In 2011, MCS voted to surrender its charter and merge with suburban Shelby County Schools, a move that ended in the suburban municipalities simply forming their own independent school districts. Recent state assessments show only about 22% of MSCS students are proficient or above in English, and 15% in math. In late 2025 and early 2026, a state forensic audit of MSCS revealed "widespread operational failures."

X
Chapter XIII1980s 2000s

The Crack Era and
Mass Incarceration

Into the vacuum created by deindustrialization, white flight, redlining, and a broken school system came the national crack cocaine epidemic of the mid-1980s. Crack hit Memphis hard. The drug economy offered one of the few remaining pathways to significant income for young men in neighborhoods that had been stripped of legitimate employment. Street gangs, some local, some affiliates of the Chicago-based Vice Lords, Gangster Disciples, and Mickey Cobras, expanded rapidly in the late 1980s and 1990s, fighting each other over territory and over the drug trade.

Memphis homicide rates, which had been high but stable through the 1960s and 1970s, spiked sharply in the late 1980s and early 1990s. The city crossed the 200-homicide threshold multiple times in the 1990s. Violent crime displaced entire neighborhoods. Insurance costs rose. Businesses closed. Residents who could leave, left. The national response, bipartisan and overwhelming, was mass incarceration. The 1986 Anti-Drug Abuse Act imposed brutally unequal sentencing for crack (disproportionately used in Black communities) versus powder cocaine (disproportionately used in white communities): a 100-to-1 ratio that took until 2010 to reduce to 18-to-1, and has still not been fully equalized. The 1994 Crime Bill added more than $9 billion for prison construction and funded 100,000 new police officers.

In Memphis and across Tennessee, state sentencing laws got harsher, mandatory minimums got longer, and the jail population exploded. A generation of young Black men, many of them arrested for nonviolent drug offenses, many of them first-time offenders, went to prison for years or decades.

"Mass incarceration did not solve the problem. It relocated the problem in time stripping a generation of men out of their families and returning them, years later, damaged, bitter, and unemployable."

Men returned from prison unable to get jobs, unable to rent apartments, unable to vote, disconnected from their children. Wives and girlfriends they had left behind had, in many cases, built lives without them. Children grew up without fathers, a strong predictor of later poverty and incarceration of their own. The 2023 Tennessee recidivism rate was 24.5% for Memphis returning citizens, reflecting the absence of meaningful re-entry support. Mass incarceration did not solve the problem. It relocated the problem in time, and it created new problems, by stripping a generation of men out of their families and communities and returning them, years later, damaged, bitter, and unemployable. Many of the young men arrested for violent crime in the 2010s and 2020s grew up without fathers because those fathers were in prison for nonviolent offenses from the 1990s.

Chapter XIV2000s Present

The Gun Economy

2×
Memphis gun theft rate vs. Detroit the next-highest city studied

One reason Memphis violence is so lethal is that it takes place in the most gun-saturated large city in the United States. Tennessee has some of the most permissive gun laws in the country. In 2021, Tennessee eliminated its permit requirement for carrying a handgun, so-called "permitless carry." Guns circulate freely through both legal and illegal markets. Private sales do not require background checks. Memphis is also the endpoint of gun trafficking pipelines running up Interstate 55 from southern Mississippi and Louisiana, and in from Arkansas via I-40.

A study by the Council on Criminal Justice found that between 2018 and 2022, Memphis had by far the highest rate of gun theft among the 16 large American cities studied, nearly twice the rate of Detroit, the next-highest city. A huge share of these thefts were guns stolen from unlocked cars. Tennessee's "constitutional carry" law contributed to a dramatic increase in the number of guns left in vehicles; when those cars were broken into or stolen (and Memphis also had one of the country's highest rates of auto theft), the guns entered the illegal market and ended up in robberies, assaults, carjackings, and homicides.

The Memphis violent crime pattern of the early 2020s was driven in substantial part by this gun theft feedback loop: stolen cars contained guns, stolen guns were used in more carjackings and robberies, those carjackings targeted more cars, which contained more guns. The cycle became self-sustaining.

Contemporary Conditions

By the 2010s, Memphis was carrying the accumulated weight of every previous era. Roughly 1 in 4 Memphians lived below the federal poverty line, and the child poverty rate for Black children in Memphis reached 36%. Median household income for white Memphians was $76,861 while Black Memphians earned $41,974, a gap of nearly $35,000 and growing. Memphis had a shortage of approximately 36,000 affordable housing units while simultaneously having between 13,000 and 15,000 vacant houses, a paradox of scarcity and abandonment produced by redlining and disinvestment. Over 16% of Memphians under 65 lacked health insurance. Only about 22% of Memphis-Shelby County Schools students were proficient in English, and 15% in math. A child born into a low-income family in North Memphis had less than 1% chance of reaching the top income quintile.

By any sociological measure, Memphis in the late 2010s and early 2020s had become the most concentrated site of urban disadvantage in America, poorer than Detroit, poorer than Baltimore, poorer than Cleveland, with worse educational outcomes and worse housing.

The COVID-19 pandemic shattered whatever fragile equilibrium remained. Unemployment spiked. Schools closed. Mental health services collapsed. Young people who had been barely holding on lost the last of their structure. Homicides climbed from roughly 250 in 2019 to 397 in 2023, the worst year in the city's modern history. Carjackings became so common that they occurred on average dozens of times per day. Rapper Young Dolph was shot and killed in November 2021 at a South Memphis cookie shop. The city made national news not for its music, not for its barbecue, not for FedEx or the Grizzlies, but for a seemingly endless parade of shootings, chases, and killings.

Chapter XVIJanuary 2023
Tyre Nichols
June 5, 1993 January 10, 2023

On the evening of January 7, 2023, Tyre Nichols, a 29-year-old Black man, was pulled over by Memphis police officers for what was described as "reckless driving" (the actual circumstances of the stop have never been clearly established). Nichols fled on foot. Five officers, members of the Memphis Police Department's SCORPION Unit, a specialized "crime suppression" team that had been formed in late 2021, caught him a few hundred feet from his mother's house.

Over the next several minutes, on video, they beat him: punching, kicking, using a police baton, pepper-spraying him. He called out for his mother. After the beating, the officers milled around, chatted, and laughed while Nichols lay slumped against a car in clear medical distress.

He died three days later, on January 10, 2023.

All five officers were fired. All five were Black. All five were charged in state court with second-degree murder and indicted on federal civil rights and witness tampering charges. The SCORPION Unit was disbanded.

In July 2023, the U.S. Department of Justice opened a pattern-and-practice investigation into the Memphis Police Department. In December 2024, the DOJ released a 73-page report with devastating findings. MPD used excessive force, particularly in encounters over minor offenses. MPD conducted unlawful stops, searches, and arrests. MPD discriminated against Black people in enforcement. MPD discriminated against people with behavioral health disabilities. MPD's SCORPION-style "saturation" strategy of flooding neighborhoods with traffic stops had led to so many unconstitutional arrests that prosecutors testified many cases were "laughed out of court." The report noted that MPD's misconduct had led to dozens of criminal cases being dismissed, meaning that police officers' unconstitutional actions were actively allowing violent criminals to go free. The City of Memphis declined to enter into a federal consent decree. In 2025, under the second Trump administration, the DOJ formally dropped the investigation and retracted its findings. The question of whether MPD will be meaningfully reformed remains open.

Chapter XVII2025 2026

The 2025 2026 Turnaround

184
Homicides in 2025 down 54% from the 2023 peak of 397
Memphis Annual Homicides, 2015 2025

The numbers are unambiguous. Homicides fell to 184 in 2025, the first time Memphis recorded fewer than 200 homicides since 2019. Overall Part I crimes dropped 27% year-over-year. Carjackings fell 48%. Motor vehicle theft fell 43%. Robbery fell 30% or more. Shootings fell 38%, meaning roughly 500 fewer Memphians were injured by gunfire in 2025 than in 2024. Early 2026 data shows the trend continuing. January 2026 Part I crimes were 1,915, down from 3,709 in January 2025. Homicides through March 23, 2026 were 11, down 65% from the same point the previous year.

Three competing claims explain the turnaround. The first is local enforcement: Memphis Police Chief CJ Davis deserves credit for targeted enforcement strategies, the expansion of camera networks through partnerships like ConnectMemphis, and focused deployments against interstate shootings. The second is a federal surge: the Trump administration's Memphis Safe Task Force, plus National Guard deployment and Rolling Thunder operations in spring 2025 that resulted in 370 arrests, 92 gang members apprehended, 59 firearms recovered, and $140,000 in cash seized. The third is a national trend: the independent Council on Criminal Justice has noted that violent crime declined in most large American cities in 2025. The question of how much of Memphis's improvement is attributable to any single intervention versus the broader national normalization post-COVID cannot be answered definitively.

What is not contested: the declines are real and meaningful. Hundreds of Memphis families who would have been at a funeral or a hospital in 2024 were spared that in 2025.

Chapter XVIII1739 2026

The Thread That
Connects It All

If you trace the line from 1739 to 2026,
a coherent story emerges.

Memphis was founded on a bluff where a European empire had tried, and failed, to exterminate the Chickasaw Nation in 1739. Eighty years later, the Chickasaws were finally dispossessed in a federal treaty negotiated by Andrew Jackson, who then personally laid out the city on land he had bought on speculation. Its economy was constructed from the ground up around the buying and selling of human beings and the extraction of unpaid labor from them. When slavery ended, the city's white establishment, backed by its police force, its courts, and its political machine, fought to preserve the racial hierarchy that cheap labor depended on. They did so through the 1866 Memphis Massacre, through the lynching of Thomas Moss and Calvin McDowell and William Stewart, through the public burning of Ell Persons, through a century of Jim Crow laws, and through the suppression of every labor movement that tried to challenge the low-wage regime the Business Men's Club of 1900 had called Memphis's "competitive advantage."

When legal segregation began to crack in the mid-20th century, capital and policy found new ways to preserve the hierarchy. Redlining maps, drawn by federal bureaucrats in 1935, determined which families would build wealth and which would not, and the maps followed race with mathematical precision. Urban renewal demolished Black neighborhoods. White flight and suburban municipal incorporation created fiscal walls. The same industrial jobs that had finally started to lift Black Memphians into the middle class, the Firestone plant, International Harvester, the rest, were gutted by the 1980s, replaced by a low-wage logistics economy that explicitly reproduced the old doctrine of cheap, disposable labor as Memphis's selling point.

Into that vacuum came concentrated poverty, a flood of guns, a drug economy, and a mass incarceration response that broke up families without fixing any of the underlying conditions. Into that vacuum came the SCORPION Unit and the beating of Tyre Nichols. Into that vacuum came 2023, and 397 homicides.

Memphis is violent because Memphis has, for most of its existence, been an extraction economy. First extraction of Chickasaw land. Then extraction of labor from enslaved people. Then extraction of labor from Jim Crow workers. Then extraction of capital and tax base from the residents who were left behind when the mobile middle class fled. Every generation of Memphians has inherited the wreckage of the generation before, and the wreckage has compounded.

The Thesis Visualized

The good news is that the 2025 to 2026 data proves change is possible. Something can be done. Homicides did fall by 54% in two years. The harder news is that whether those gains last, and whether the next generation of Memphis children grows up in a city that has finally broken the pattern, or a city that is merely between crime waves, will depend on whether Memphis, and the state of Tennessee, and the federal government, finally address the 200-year-old conditions underneath the crime numbers.

None of the structural conditions described in this report have been fixed. The poverty is still there. The wage gap is still there. The housing shortage is still there. The school outcomes are still there. The Black-to-white wealth gap traceable to the 1935 redlining maps is still there. The concentrated disadvantage in North and South Memphis is still there.

Policing without investment is a Band-Aid. Investment without policing is a dream. Memphis has the rare chance, right now, to pair both with a serious reckoning with its history. Enforcement can suppress the symptoms. It cannot cure the disease. History teaches that crime booms and busts. The United States has gone through at least three major urban crime spikes and declines since 1960. But the underlying social conditions that produce the next spike are always still in place unless they are deliberately addressed. Whether Memphis will take that step, remains to be seen.
